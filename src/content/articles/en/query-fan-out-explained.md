Ask Google's AI Mode a long question and it does not simply search your sentence. Google says it can run several related searches at once and combine the results into one answer. The technique is called query fan-out. A lot of advice built on it is guesswork, so this guide separates what Google has documented from what it has not.

## What Google documents

Google's page "AI features and your website" says AI Overviews and AI Mode may run several related searches across subtopics and data sources while they build a response. As they do, they can find more supporting pages, which Google says lets them show "a wider and more diverse set of helpful links" than classic search. The word to notice is "may". Google also says AI Overviews appear only when its systems judge them additive to classic Search, so they often do not trigger. The two features may also use different models and techniques, so the links they show can differ.

Google's separate AI optimisation guide, updated on 10 July 2026, defines fan-out as "A set of concurrent, related queries generated by the model".

## What a Google executive has added

In a July 2025 interview reported by Search Engine Journal, Google's Robby Stein described a language model reading the question and creating related searches, some on topics the user never mentioned. His example was a query about things to do in Nashville with a group, which could produce sub-questions about restaurants, bars and activities for children. For Deep Search, a heavier mode, his account points to dozens or even hundreds of background searches over several minutes. That is an interview, not a technical specification, so treat the detail as illustrative.

## What is not documented

We found no Google document that says:

- how many sub-searches an ordinary answer runs;
- how the sub-searches are written, or how results are ranked and merged;
- whether the same question produces the same sub-searches each time.

Nor does Search Console list them. Its generative AI report counts impressions by page, country, device and date, with no query breakdown. Our inference: any tool that claims to show Google's real sub-queries is estimating them.

## What Google says to do, and not to do

- **No extra requirements.** A page must be indexed and eligible to appear in Search with a snippet. Google says no special optimisation or new markup is needed, and recommends standard SEO fundamentals.
- **No page per query.** Google advises against making a separate page for every query variation, fan-out queries included, when the aim is to manipulate rankings or AI responses. It says that "violates Google's scaled content abuse spam policy", and that a large number of pages does not make a site better.
- **No need for llms.txt.** Google says Search does not use it and that it neither helps nor harms visibility.

## A sensible response (our view)

None of this is Google's advice beyond the points above. It is how we would apply them.

1. **List the follow-up questions.** After a reader gets the main answer, what would they ask next? For a page on choosing an air fryer in Malaysia, that might be size, wattage, price range, warranty and servicing. The example is ours.
2. **Answer them on the page,** under clear headings, instead of splitting them into thin pages. Create a separate page only when the topic would serve a reader alone.
3. **Check what AI features already use.** In Search Console's [generative AI report](/ai-search/search-console-generative-ai-report/), sort pages by impressions.
4. **Test it yourself.** Where AI Mode is available, ask your own question and note which subtopics the answer covers.

## A counterpoint

Some SEOs say fan-out explains why pages that do not rank for the exact query still get cited. Google's documentation says only that the technique can surface a wider set of links, not that rank stops mattering. Google also says clicks from AI Overviews tend to be higher quality, though the page we read gives no figures, and Search Console's AI report counts impressions, not clicks. For the evidence on rankings and citations, see [GEO vs SEO in 2026](/ai-search/geo-vs-seo-2026/).

## What we do not know

We do not know the number of sub-searches, how they are chosen, or how stable they are from one day to the next. We found no Google statement on Malay or mixed Malay and English queries. And we have no evidence that structuring a page around guessed sub-queries raises its chance of being cited. Treat that as a hypothesis to test on your own pages, not a rule.
