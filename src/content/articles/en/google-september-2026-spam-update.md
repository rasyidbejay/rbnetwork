Google marked its September 2026 spam update complete on 8 October, after nearly two weeks. The useful questions now are narrower than the headlines: what has Google actually said, what is third-party chatter, and how should a site owner read their own numbers before changing anything?

This piece separates what is confirmed from what is not. Where we are reading between the lines, we say so.

## What Google has confirmed

Google's Search Status Dashboard gives the core facts:

- **Dates.** The update began at 9:15 a.m. Pacific on 24 September 2026. The incident ended on 8 October, and the completion note was posted at 1:37 a.m. Pacific. That is about 13 days and 16 hours. In Malaysia time it runs from just after midnight on 25 September to the afternoon of 8 October.
- **Scope.** Google said it applies globally and to all languages, and that the rollout might take up to two weeks.
- **Context.** It is the fourth spam update of 2026, after March (24 to 25 March), June (24 to 26 June) and August (18 to 21 August), according to Search Engine Roundtable's list. The first three each finished within about three days, so the length is what stands out.

## What Google has not said

Google has not said what the update targets. The dashboard entry gives dates and scope and nothing more, and we found no companion explanation. Articles that claim it is "about" AI content, parasite SEO or link networks are going beyond what Google published.

Google's documentation describes spam updates in general: a notable improvement to the automated systems that detect spam, such as its AI-based SpamBrain. Sites that see a change are told to review the spam policies. Improvement may come only after the systems learn, over a period of months, that a site complies. Google calls this a spam update, not a link spam update. For link spam updates the documentation says lost ranking benefit cannot be regained, so the label matters.

## Reported by others, not confirmed by Google

Search Engine Roundtable logged ranking volatility on 25 to 27 September, around 30 September, and 4 to 6 October. That comes from tracking tools and community chatter, and Google has confirmed none of it. Some SEOs said the update felt like a core update. The author who reported this said he was not sure about the comparison, and Google has not called it a core update. Treat these as prompts to check your own data, not as findings.

## Does it apply in Malaysia?

Google said "all languages", so Malay is covered by its own wording. No source we found names Malaysia or Malay, and we found no Malaysia-specific impact data. Our reading is that Malaysian sites are in scope in the same way as everyone else, and nothing more than that.

## How to read your own data

Google's guide to debugging traffic drops lists the usual causes: algorithmic updates, technical issues, security issues, spam issues, seasonality and site moves. It suggests comparing date ranges in the Search Console Performance report, checking every tab (queries, pages, countries, devices) and looking back 16 months for yearly patterns. It also says a small move, such as from position 2 to 4, is normal fluctuation.

One caution on freshness. On 11 October, Search Engine Roundtable saw the Performance report more than 64 hours behind, and reported it back to normal on 12 October. Google had not commented. Our reading is that a lagging chart can make the latest days look like a fall, so check the date of the last data point before you read a trend.

## Five checks

1. **Fix the window in your own time zone.** Google's times are Pacific, so for Malaysia the window is roughly 25 September to 8 October. On timing alone, a drop that began well before or well after is unlikely to be this update. That is our reading.
2. **Check Manual actions and Security issues first.** Google lists these as separate causes. The spam policies page we read does not say that sites are told about algorithmic demotions, so a clean Manual actions report does not rule the update out.
3. **Compare like with like.** Compare the window with an equal-length period before it, and line up the same weekdays so a weekend does not distort the picture. The weekday point is our suggestion, not Google's.
4. **Look for patterns, not totals.** Did one section, page type or template fall, or the whole site? Then compare those pages with the spam policies. For a content site, scaled content abuse, scraping, doorway abuse and thin affiliation are the ones to read first. That is our judgement.
5. **Rule out the rest.** Check Crawl stats and Page indexing for technical faults, Google Trends for seasonality, and whether you changed templates, plugins or hosting in the same weeks. Do not rewrite pages in reaction to a daily wobble, because Google says improvement takes months.

## What we still do not know

We do not know which spam tactics or sites were affected. We do not know whether Google will refresh the update: some commentary expects it, but the Google pages we read do not say so. Nobody outside Google can say whether a core update follows, and speculation is not evidence. We also have no data on how Malay-language or Malaysian sites fared. We will update this piece if Google says more.
