A directory of AI-written pages on facebook.com is reportedly showing up in Google at very large scale. The label some people have put on it is a judgement, not a finding. This piece separates the two and applies Google's own test.

## What the pages are

Facebook Answers pages sit under facebook.com/fb-answers/. Each is an AI-written summary of a topic, built from public Facebook posts and pages. Search Engine Roundtable reported on 5 November that Google has indexed pages from the directory and that they appear in results.

Two people are credited with spotting it. Saul Westbrook of Peripheral Creative says he noticed in June 2026 that Meta had quietly got millions of these pages indexed. Glenn Gabe says the directory began filling in July, and shows pages ranking for millions of queries in standard results, AI Overviews and AI Mode.

## What the figures are worth

The roughly 6 million figure comes from a screenshot of a check against Google's index, and the report does not say which query was used. Westbrook also shared Semrush traffic estimates for the directory, but the report does not print them, and we have not verified any traffic figure. Treat all of these as rough. Anyone outside a company can only estimate how many of its pages Google holds, which is why we call the 6 million an order of magnitude and not a count.

## What Google's policy actually tests

Google's spam policies, last updated on 28 August 2026, define scaled content abuse as mass-producing pages mainly to game rankings rather than to help people. The policy applies however the pages are made. The question it asks is purpose, and automation alone is not the violation. Using generative AI to produce many pages that add nothing for users is a listed example.

That leaves two questions that cannot be answered from outside. What was the purpose of the pages? And do they add value, given that they draw on real posts written by real people?

## Why this is a hard case

Our reading, with both sides stated. A defender could say each page summarises what real people posted, which is more than rewording another website. A critic could say a templated summary of a topic, repeated at a scale of millions, is exactly the pattern the policy describes. Neither argument is a fact about what Google will do.

## What we are not claiming

- We are not claiming Google will act, or that it will not. Barry Schwartz expects the pages not to stay indexed for long, which is his view.
- We are not repeating monthly visit estimates, because we could not check them.
- We are not saying AI-written content is penalised. Google's policy says otherwise.

## What it means for a small publisher

One case on a very large domain tells you little about a small site. The safer reading of the policy is the plain one: each page should answer a real question, carry claims you can source, and not be a template stamped out in bulk. Three checks of our own before publishing a page:

- Does it answer a question a real person has?
- Can every claim be traced to a source you can name?
- Would you still publish it if you were only making ten pages, not ten thousand?

Our [September spam update piece](/seo/google-september-2026-spam-update/) covers how to read your own data, and [our AI-built site checklist](/web-development/ai-built-website-seo/) covers the basics.

## What would change our view

A statement from Google, the pages dropping out of the index, or a manual action reported against the directory. Any of these would give the debate a fact to stand on.
